Tamil Nadu sends 39 members to the Lok Sabha. That number was fixed in 1976 and has not moved since, because Parliament froze seat allocation on the 1971 census and then extended the freeze twice.

The freeze runs out after 2026. A Constitution Amendment Bill to replace it was voted down in April, and the government is [reported to be preparing a second attempt](https://www.businesstoday.in/india/story/special-parliament-session-likely-from-aug-17-for-delimitation-womens-quota-bills-sources-547354-2026-08-05) at a special session this month, this time with an assurance attached: that every state's seats would rise by 50 percent.

So the question is live, and each option on the table has a clear answer. This piece works through them, and it spends most of its time on that 50 percent, because the phrase is being used for two different proposals that are ten seats apart for Tamil Nadu.

## The short answer

{{< kpi-row >}}
{{< kpi value="39" label="Tamil Nadu today" tone="muted" sub="fixed on the 1971 census, unchanged since 1976" >}}
{{< kpi value="32" label="if the House stays at 543" tone="warn" sub="a loss of 7 seats" >}}
{{< kpi value="51" label="if the House grows to 850" tone="good" sub="a gain of 12 seats" >}}
{{< kpi value="58 or 48" label="under a '50% increase'" tone="accent" sub="depending on which version you mean" >}}
{{< /kpi-row >}}

**Tamil Nadu only loses seats if the House stays the same size.** Every enlargement now on the table gives it more seats than it has today.

That is a fact about those proposals rather than about enlargement itself. If seats are shared out by population, Tamil Nadu does not get back to its present **39** until the House reaches **656**, so a smaller enlargement would still leave it short.

Whether any of them protects Tamil Nadu's **share** of Parliament is a separate question, and it has a different answer depending on which proposal you mean. That distinction is the whole argument, and the phrase doing the most damage to it is "a 50 percent increase", which is currently being used for two incompatible things.

## The options on the table, side by side

These use the 2011 census, which is the one the Bill names, on the **2011** territorial **map**, under the carve-out **model** explained in the [method note](/posts/how-india-seat-rule-method-note/). They are **hypothetical**: India has not moved seats between states since 1976.

| What happens | House size | Tamil Nadu | Change | TN's share |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Nothing changes | 543 | 39 | none | 7.18% |
| Seats recomputed on population | 543 | **32** | **-7** | 5.89% |
| House grows until no state loses | 709 | 42 | +3 | 5.92% |
| Every state's own seats multiplied by 1.5 | 806 | **58** | **+19** | **7.20%** |
| House up 50%, then shared out by population | 815 | 48 | +9 | 5.89% |
| The 2026 Bill as introduced | 850 | **51** | **+12** | 6.00% |
| Cube root of the population | 1,066 | 64 | +25 | 6.00% |

**The 2026 Bill** raises the ceiling to 850 members, with up to 815 from the states and 35 from the union territories. It was introduced on 16 April and negatived on 17 April, 298 votes to 230, short of the 352 needed for a two-thirds majority.

The two rows in the middle are the ones to slow down on. They differ by ten seats, and in the current debate **both of them are called a 50 percent increase**.

## What "a 50 percent increase" actually means

There are two versions in circulation and they are not variations on one idea. They are opposite answers to the question this whole argument is about.

**Version one: multiply every state's own seats by 1.5.** Tamil Nadu's 39 becomes 58. Uttar Pradesh's 80 becomes 120. Kerala's 20 becomes 30. Because every state grows by the same proportion, **every state keeps the share of Parliament it holds today**. Tamil Nadu stays at about 7.2%.

This is the version being described in public. NCP (SP) MP Supriya Sule offered conditional support for a delimitation bill on this basis in July, and said the formula had been put to an all-party meeting by Amit Shah and Kiren Rijiju. The TDP has backed it. [Outlook's explainer](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/supriya-sules-delimitation-formula-explained-why-a-uniform-50-seat-increase-has-entered-the-debate) of the formula gives the same arithmetic I do: Tamil Nadu about 58, Maharashtra 72, Uttar Pradesh 120.

**Version two: enlarge the House by about 50 percent, then share the seats out by population.** Tamil Nadu gets 48. This is the column on the [PRS bill tracker](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-constitution-131st-amendment-bill-2026), and I rebuilt their model independently and got 48 as well, and 32 in their unchanged-House column.

**Ten seats and 1.3 points of share separate them.** If you have seen the promise of a 50% rise and assumed it meant Tamil Nadu keeps its weight in Parliament, you were reading version one. The published projections are all version two.

### Which one would the law produce?

Version two. The Wire called the uniform version [a statutory ghost](https://thewire.in/government/explained-why-the-promised-50-increase-in-lok-sabha-is-mathematical-impossibility-under-the-delimitation-bill), absent from the bills and inconsistent with them. I went and read the Bill, and the text bears that out:

- **Article 81(2)(a)** requires that seats be allotted so the ratio between a state's seats and its population is "so far as practicable, the same for all States".
- **Section 8 of the [Delimitation Bill, 2026](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/bills_parliament/2026/Delimitation_Bill,_2026.pdf)** binds the Commission to determine each state's seats "on the basis of the latest census figures and having regard to the provisions of articles 81, 82".
- The Bill never names a census. **Section 2(e)** defines the latest census figures as those "published as on the date of the constitution of the Commission", which on any near-term timetable means 2011.

You cannot hold that ratio equal across states and also hand every state the same percentage rise, because state populations have not grown at the same rate since 1971. A uniform increase is not a way of implementing the Bill. It is a different policy, and putting it into law would mean amending article 81(2) itself, not merely lifting the freeze that sits in article 82. That is a larger constitutional change than the one that was voted down in April, and I have not found a published draft of it.

### What version one actually buys, and what it costs

{{< chart src="t5-two-readings" wide="true"
   alt="People per MP for seven states under three regimes. Today the bars are uneven, from 1.67 million in Kerala to 2.74 million in Rajasthan. Multiplying every state by 1.5 keeps them uneven, from 1.11 million to 1.85 million. Sharing seats by population makes every bar level at about 1.5 million."
   caption="The middle bars are the uniform increase. They are smaller than today's, and just as unequal to each other." >}}

A uniform increase protects Tamil Nadu's share, and it protects it by **keeping the imbalance the share is made of**.

Multiply everyone by 1.5 and a Kerala MP represents **1.11 million** people while an Uttar Pradesh MP represents **1.67 million**, a gap of about five and a half lakh per constituency. Those are The Wire's figures, and my own calculation lands on the same two numbers. Both are unaffected by the rounding wrinkle below, since Kerala and Uttar Pradesh both hold an even number of seats.

The widest gap between large states does not close at all. It is **1.64 today**, and **between 1.62 and 1.66 after a uniform rise** depending on how the half seats are settled, against **1.14** if the same number of seats is shared out by population instead.

So the choice is not between a fair option and an unfair one. It is between two different definitions of fair, and each buys one at the price of the other:

- **Equal weight per state.** Tamil Nadu's voice in Parliament stays where it is. The cost is that an Indian's vote goes on counting for different amounts in different states, and the gap keeps widening for as long as growth rates differ.
- **Equal weight per person.** Every MP represents roughly the same number of people, for the first time in India's history. The cost is that Tamil Nadu's share falls to about 6%, and states are effectively rebased on demographic outcomes they cannot now change.

There is no version that delivers both. That is not a drafting failure, it is arithmetic.

{{< callout title="A wrinkle nobody has priced" type="insight" >}}
A uniform 50% rise does not produce a whole number of seats. **17 of the 35 states and union territories hold an odd number of seats today**, so multiplying by 1.5 lands each of them on a fraction of exactly one half. Tamil Nadu is one: 39 x 1.5 = **58.5**.

India's own rule, written by the Delimitation Commission in 1963, rounds to the nearest whole number and gives any spare seat to "the largest fraction **less than** one-half". It says nothing about a fraction that **is** one-half. Round all 17 down and the House is **806**; round them all up and it is **823**. Tamil Nadu gets 58 or 59, and nothing on paper decides which.

Half the country would sit on that silence. The rounding rule is the subject of [the longer piece](/posts/the-rule-india-wrote-down-in-1963/) this one comes from.
{{< /callout >}}

## If seats follow population, House size makes no difference

Set the uniform-increase idea aside for a moment, because everything in this section assumes seats are shared out by population, which is what the bills as drafted require.

Tamil Nadu is **5.96% of India's people**. It holds **7.18% of the seats**. That 1.22-point gap is what fifty years of a frozen census bought: Tamil Nadu's population grew slower than the country's, and its seat count did not adjust.

Now look at what happens to that share as the House grows.

{{< chart src="t2-tn-share" wide="true"
   alt="Tamil Nadu's share of Lok Sabha seats plotted against House size from 543 to 1,100. The line stays near 6%, close to Tamil Nadu's 5.96% share of India's population, far below the 7.18% share it holds today."
   caption="The jagged line is the rounding. What matters is that it never climbs back to the upper dashed line, at any House size." >}}

**It lands on about 6% and stays there.** At 709, at 850, at 1,100. That is not a quirk of any particular proposal. It is what proportional allocation means: each state's share converges on its share of the population, and the size of the House does not enter into it.

PRS puts the same point in one sentence:

{{< pullquote >}}
The change in the relative seat share will be maintained even if the total seats in Lok Sabha is increased.
{{< /pullquote >}}

So enlarging Parliament changes one thing and not the other. **It converts an absolute loss into an absolute gain. It does nothing to the proportion.**

That is worth being precise about, because both sides of the argument have used the ambiguity. "Tamil Nadu will lose seats" is false if the House grows. "Tamil Nadu loses nothing" is false too, as long as seats are shared out by population, because share is what determines influence in a vote.

The only way to hold the share is to stop allocating by population, which is exactly what version one does and exactly why it does not fit inside the current Bill.

## Why this is happening to Tamil Nadu

Not because of the rounding rule, and not because of any paradox. Because of arithmetic that is easy to state.

Between **1971** and 2011, with the newer states **folded** back into their parents so the comparison is like for like:

{{< chart src="t4-growth" wide="true"
   alt="Population growth from 1971 to 2011 by state. Kerala grew 57% and Tamil Nadu 75%, against India's 121%. Rajasthan grew 166%, Bihar 143% and Uttar Pradesh 138%."
   caption="Every state below the dashed line loses share under any proportional rule. Every state above it gains." >}}

Tamil Nadu grew **75%**. India grew **121%**. Kerala grew **57%**, the slowest of the large states. Rajasthan grew **166%**, Bihar **143%**, Uttar Pradesh **138%**.

A state that grows at roughly half the national rate ends up with a smaller share of a population-based House. There is no trick in it, and no way of dividing seats by population avoids it, which is the subject of [the longer piece](/posts/the-rule-india-wrote-down-in-1963/) this one comes from.

The uniform increase escapes it only by refusing the premise: it does not divide seats by population at all.

{{< callout title="The thing most often got wrong" type="insight" >}}
Tamil Nadu is not losing share because of the way seats are rounded, and not because of any mathematical paradox. It is losing share because it grew more slowly than the rest of India. That is proportional representation working exactly as intended. Whether it is *fair* is a real argument. Whether it is a *malfunction* is not: it isn't one.
{{< /callout >}}

## The argument Tamil Nadu rarely makes

There is a fact in Tamil Nadu's favour that almost never appears in the coverage, and it is about constituencies rather than share.

{{< chart src="t3-people-per-mp-tn" wide="true"
   alt="People per MP for seven states under three scenarios. Today the range runs from 1.67 million in Kerala to 2.74 million in Rajasthan. At a House of 850 every state sits near 1.4 million."
   caption="At 850 seats every large state converges on about 1.4 million people per MP, and every one of them is better off than today." >}}

A Tamil Nadu MP today represents about **1.85 million** people. If the House stays at 543 and seats are recomputed, that rises to **2.25 million**, because Tamil Nadu has fewer MPs for the same population. But if the House grows to 850, it **falls to 1.41 million**.

**A bigger Parliament gives Tamil Nadu smaller constituencies than it has now**, not larger ones. Every large state converges on roughly 1.4 million people per MP, which is the closest India would have come to equal representation in its history. Those figures use 2011 population, so they are a floor; the real numbers in 2026 are higher everywhere.

## The arguments, at their strongest

I am not going to tell you which side is right. Here is what each would say if it were putting its own case well.

**For enlarging and sharing by population.** No state loses a seat, so nothing has to be taken from anyone and the change is politically survivable. Tamil Nadu gets 12 more MPs and smaller constituencies. The under-representation of the northern states is real and has festered for fifty years: Uttar Pradesh goes from 80 to 140 under the Bill. A vote in Rajasthan currently counts for a good deal less than a vote in Kerala, which is hard to defend on any democratic principle. And the freeze cannot run indefinitely without the distortion compounding.

**Against it.** Share is what decides votes, and the Bill concedes the share while protecting the count, which is the symbol rather than the substance. PRS notes structural effects that have nothing to do with region: the Lok Sabha to Rajya Sabha ratio moves from **2.2:1 to 3.3:1**, weakening the Council of States in presidential elections and joint sittings; the cap on the Council of Ministers is 15% of the House, so it rises from 81 to 122; and each MP gets less floor time, since questions are balloted. The Bill also lets Parliament choose which census to use by simple majority, replacing a constitutional rule with a political one.

**For the uniform increase.** It is the only proposal on the table that leaves the federal balance where it is, and federations routinely decline to allocate power strictly by headcount: the Rajya Sabha was meant to do this job in India and does not, because its seats also track population. A state that met a national policy objective should not have its parliamentary weight cut for having met it. Everyone still gets a smaller constituency than they have today, so nobody is worse off in absolute terms.

**Against the uniform increase.** It freezes the 1971 distortion permanently rather than for another twenty-five years, and does so at the moment the freeze was due to end. It cannot be done under article 81(2) as written, so it needs a larger constitutional change than the one Parliament has already rejected, and no draft of it has been published so far as I can find. It leaves an Indian's vote worth different amounts in different states indefinitely. And because the ratio it locks in keeps drifting, the same argument returns at the next census with a wider gap to defend.

**And the argument that is really about something else.** Tamil Nadu's case is often put as reward and punishment: the state did what national policy asked on family planning and is being penalised for succeeding. That is a claim about fairness between states, not about arithmetic, and no seat formula settles it. The measures that would address it directly, a reformed Rajya Sabha with fixed state representation, or delinking fiscal devolution from population, are in neither version.

{{< callout title="What would actually change the answer" type="method" >}}
Nothing in the seat formula. Tamil Nadu's share follows its population share under every method tested here, and the seven classical apportionment methods differ by at most two seats for any state.

The one proposal that does hold Tamil Nadu's share is the uniform increase, and it manages it by **not using a seat formula at all**: it multiplies the existing table and never consults a census. If Tamil Nadu wants to hold influence above its population share, that is where it has to come from, and it is a constitutional argument rather than a mathematical one.
{{< /callout >}}

{{< callout title="Where the numbers come from" type="method" >}}
Populations are the 2011 census, Table A-2. Current seats are the 2008 Delimitation Order. Allocations use the rule the Delimitation Commission set out in 1963: divide by the average, round to the nearest whole number, and give any spare seat to the largest fraction below one half.

All figures are **hypothetical** and depend on a **model**: which units sit outside the proportional pool. The default holds the 15 sub-six-million states and union territories at their current seats. Tamil Nadu's figures are the most robust in the set, coming out at 32 and 48 under both this model and the different one PRS uses.

**The uniform-increase row is the exception, and it is the simplest number here.** It takes the 2008 seat table and multiplies it by 1.5, so it uses no census, no divisor, no pool and no model. The only judgement in it is what to do with the 17 units that land on exactly half a seat, which is why it is quoted as a range.

**Units are the 2011 map**, which is not today's map: Andhra Pradesh here is **undivided**, and Jammu & Kashmir is pre-2019. Several **territorial** changes since 2011 are not reflected.

One thing worth flagging about the most-cited projection. **PRS** publishes its own state-by-state table, and it does not sum to a valid House: the unchanged column totals 548 against a House of 543, and the 50% column totals 813 against 814.5. PRS **flags** this **themselves**, as "rounding off for each state". I tried five reconstructions of their arithmetic and none reaches 548, so I cannot say what they did. The agreement that matters is that on Tamil Nadu we get the same answer either way.
